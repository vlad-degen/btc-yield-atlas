Earning yield on Bitcoin: where the money is, and what borrowing against it really pays

We counted every product that pays a yield on BTC, followed each category for two years, and took apart the five largest products that borrow dollars against Bitcoin to earn it. This is the short version for the team. Details sit in the tabs and fold-outs; the sources are listed under Data and in the repository.

What this means for us

  1. The demand is real and it comes through distribution. Kraken raised $527M on a product paying under 2%, and its deposits did not follow the rate. Whoever owns the app owns the flow.
  2. The yield is thin and mostly paid by someone else. Without the stablecoin rewards (the funder is not disclosed; most likely Ripple and PayPal), Kraken would pay about 0.1–0.6%. Bitget's yield is Bitget's own money. Anything above 1% needs cheaper funding, collateral that earns by itself, or named credit risk.
  3. The leaders cut corners we can avoid cheaply. Wrappers on a single key, 1-hour timelocks, two days to cut the loan after a fall, and no split between rewards and real yield. See the Playbook.

2 · How carry works

The trade in four steps

One loan against your Bitcoin, no re-borrowing. Each step has its own way to fail.

STEP 1
You hand over BTC
It is turned into a token on another blockchain (kBTC, cbBTC, WBTC) and put into a vault. You get vault shares; their price in BTC is your real yield.
Can fail: whoever controls the token. kBTC is controlled by one Kraken key.
STEP 2
The vault borrows dollars
It posts the BTC token as collateral in a lending market and borrows stablecoins worth about 60 to 65% of it.
Can fail: BTC falls 18 to 35% and the collateral is sold; or the market runs out of dollars and the rate jumps.
STEP 3
It parks the dollars
In a lending vault, a loan token or a trading strategy that pays more than the loan costs. The gap is the profit.
Can fail: the parked asset loses value. That is what broke at Stream Finance in November 2025 and at Resolv in March 2026.
STEP 4
It buys BTC with the profit
The surplus dollars are swapped back into BTC, so each share is worth a little more BTC over time. The operator takes a cut.
Watch: the fee. At Kraken the vault's operators keep 25% of a yield of about 1.4% before fees.

Formula: collateral yield + (parking rate − loan rate) × LTV × share posted × (1 − fee). Liquidation buffer = 1 − LTV ÷ threshold. One loan, no re-borrowing.

Words we use
Carry
Earning the gap between the rate you borrow at and the rate you lend at.
Wrapper
A token on another blockchain that stands for BTC held by someone: kBTC (Kraken), cbBTC (Coinbase), WBTC (BitGo), LBTC (Lombard).
LTV
Loan-to-value: the loan divided by the collateral. 64% means $64 borrowed per $100 of BTC.
Liquidation threshold
The LTV at which the market sells your collateral to repay the loan: 86% in Kraken's markets.
Utilization
How much of a lending pool is lent out. Above 90% the borrow rate jumps; at 100% it is four times the normal rate.
Curator
The firm that decides where a vault's money goes and sets its risk limits: Sentora for Kraken, Gauntlet for Bitget.
Share price
How much BTC one vault share is worth. Its growth is the yield you actually got, after fees.
Rewards, incentives
Extra payments from a token issuer or a blockchain on top of normal interest, to attract users. They can stop at any time.
Timelock
A delay before an admin change takes effect, so users can leave first. It only protects you if it is longer than the time it takes to withdraw.
Multisig
An account that needs several keys to act. "1-of-1" means one key is enough.
EOA
A plain wallet run by one private key, with no multisig or code rules.
Health factor
The liquidation threshold divided by the current LTV. Below 1.0 the loan can be liquidated.
NAV
Net asset value: what the vault's holdings are worth, per share or in total.
Same block
Within one blockchain transaction, so in seconds and with no waiting.

How it works

  1. A Kraken user taps Allocate. The app's built-in wallet puts BTC, as kBTC, into the vault on Ink.
  2. The vault bridges the kBTC to Ethereum and posts it on Morpho. It borrows RLUSD and PYUSD at about 3%, plus some USDT against WBTC on Aave and Morpho. Debt: $298M.
  3. Two thirds of the dollars go back into Sentora's own lender vaults, which pay about 6% including RLUSD and PYUSD rewards (funder not disclosed). One third goes into PRIME, a token backed by Figure home-equity loans, at about 6–7%.
  4. The profit is swapped back to kBTC. The share price rises at most 0.5% per update, at most every 6 hours. The vault takes a 25% fee on the gain (20% before 24 August), split 80/15/5 between three addresses; the 15% goes to Sentora.

Worth copying

  • Distribution inside an exchange app: one tap, built-in wallets, gas paid, BTC purchase inside the deposit
  • A BTC share price that moves in small, bounded steps with a high-water-mark fee
  • Several borrow markets and a clear order for paying the loan back
  • Separating subsidized, liquid parking from credit that is slow to exit

Not worth copying

  • 82% of the carry from rewards while saying it is not a subsidy
  • Borrowing from one curator's capped vault; the vault itself caused 5 of the 7 rate spikes above 6% (peaks up to 13.75%)
  • Running at the target loan-to-value and taking about 2 days to reduce it after a fall
  • 31% of the debt in a token only an operator can turn back into cash
  • Quiet fee and penalty changes; the wrapper on a single key

How it works

  1. You deposit BTC. The protocol borrows the same value in crvUSD from a credit line that Curve's DAO minted for it (up to 1B crvUSD, no interest paid to Curve).
  2. Both go into a Curve BTC/crvUSD pool. The position is twice your deposit, half of it borrowed, so the pool's usual loss when BTC moves is cancelled out, in theory.
  3. Traders keep the leverage at 2x by arbitrage; nobody is liquidated. The "interest" is paid back into the pool to fund that rebalancing.
  4. Unstaked shares earn the trading fees. Staked shares give up the fees and earn the YB token instead.

How it works

  1. Bitget users swap BTC for BGBTC inside the exchange. Bitget holds the BTC in one address (1,420 BTC, 120% of supply) and mints the token.
  2. One Bitget wallet deposits 801.6 bgBTC into a Gauntlet vault on Morph. It borrows $43M USDC on Morpho.
  3. The USDC goes into Gauntlet's USDC vault, which lends it straight back to the same market. The vault owns 82% of it. It is a closed loop.
  4. The profit is a weekly USDC reward of about $39,000, paid from Bitget's own hot wallet. The vault swaps it into bgBTC and adds it to its collateral.

Worth copying

  • Aera's accounting: hourly share price inside a ±1% band
  • A tight loan-to-value band managed in both directions (62–64%)
  • A dollar vault that needs both partners to sign for changes, with 7-day delays
  • Proof of reserves plus an API that matches the exchange's own number to the satoshi

Not worth copying

  • An oracle that cannot see the wrapper lose its backing; almost no market to sell seized collateral

7 · Playbook

If we build one: what to build, how to run it, how to grow it

What the five products and the three closed ones teach, turned into a design, a risk policy with numbers, a partner list and a growth plan.

The short version

  1. Carry pays about 0.1 to 0.6% on its own. Borrowing dollars against BTC costs about 3 to 4.8%; safe places to park them pay 3.5–5%. Everything above 1% comes from cheaper funding, collateral that earns by itself, or credit risk. Say which.
  2. The risk is speed and liquidity, not the BTC price. How fast you cut the loan after a fall, how much debt you can repay in the same block, and how long a bridge or operator takes.
  3. Distribution grows the product, not the rate. Kraken's deposits did not follow its yield. Rewards cost 1.3–4% of TVL a year and stop working when they stop.

What grew TVL

  • A button in an app people already use. Kraken: 48,776 wallets and $436M of net deposits in four months. Tiny minimum, BTC purchase inside the deposit, gas paid. Deposits did not follow the rate.
  • A launch that feels like an event. "$30M in 10 hours"; "pools filled in a minute". Caps raised in steps create scarcity and prove demand.
  • An institutional channel without a wrapper. Maple found an estimated 50 to 66 clients, typically 10 to 20 BTC each, until the strategy failed.

What did not

  • Rewards without distribution. Bitget pays 1.7–2.6% of TVL a year and stopped growing after three weeks. Yield Basis paid $11M in tokens and is 45% below its peak.
  • Points. Acre and Hyperbeat.
  • Integrations promised, not delivered. Kraken grew without a single DeFi integration.
  • Quiet fee rises. Kraken's first week of outflows was the week its fee went up.

A growth plan

  1. Sign a distributor first: an exchange, a wallet or a neobank.
  2. Sign a direct programme with a stablecoin issuer, and show it as a separate line in the yield.
  3. Launch with a public schedule of cap raises.
  4. Publish a live dashboard: yield without rewards, loan-to-value, repayment ladder, who pays what.
  5. After $100M: make the share usable as collateral on Morpho and list it on Pendle for DeFi users.
Three ways past 1%, and what we would not copy
Option A

Cheaper funding, disclosed

Borrowing at 1% instead of 3% roughly doubles the yield. The routes are a direct issuer programme with a published size and end date, or a protocol credit line like Curve's for Yield Basis. The catch: someone pays, and the product must still work without it.

Option B

Collateral that earns on its own

LBTC now earns from covered calls run by Bitwise. Posted as collateral, it adds 0.3–1% on top of the carry. The catch: small markets to borrow against LBTC, plus option and custodian risk.

Option C

Win on transparency

Publish every position and key, the share of yield that is rewards and who pays it, and how full the borrow caps are. Timelocks longer than the exit, an oracle that checks the wrapper. It costs little and nobody does it.

What we would not copy. Funding the loan from our own lender vault (the Sentora and Gauntlet loops). Parking dollars in a token that only an operator can turn back into cash beyond what the ladder covers. A wrapper on a single key, or an oracle that cannot see it lose its backing. An admin timelock shorter than the withdrawal period. A 25% fee on a yield near 1%, or changing fees without notice. Book value that hides what an exit would pay.

Five questions to ask any BTC vault

What has the share price done? Not the APY on the page.
Which stablecoin does it borrow, and what is the yield without rewards? If it borrows RLUSD or PYUSD, probably close to zero.
Where exactly do the dollars go? A lender vault, a loan token, a trade. "DeFi strategies" is not an answer.
How much of the loan can it repay in the same block, and how full are the borrow caps?
Who holds the keys, and is the timelock longer than the exit?
